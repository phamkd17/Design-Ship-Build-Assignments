How a Gas Turbine Works

Stage 1 — Intake

Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan — intake and fan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

01

Air enters
the engine

Atmospheric air is drawn in through the front inlet. The intake duct slows and smooths the airflow. In a turbofan, a large fan at the front accelerates huge volumes of air before it reaches the compressor.

1,000 kg/s mass flow Ambient pressure
Stage 2 — Compression

Axial compressor — alternating rotor and stator blade rows. (Wikimedia Commons)

02

Squeezed to
40× pressure

Rotating blades (rotors) and fixed blades (stators) alternate in the compressor, each stage squeezing the air a little more. High pressure means more energy from every kilogram of fuel burned.

40× pressure ratio ~600°C exit temp
Stage 3 — Combustion

Combustor of a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

03

Continuous
flame

Fuel is injected and ignites continuously — not in pulses like a car engine. The flame burns at over 1,500°C. Hollow blades and ceramic coatings protect the turbine from the extreme heat downstream.

1,500°C flame temp Continuous burn
Stage 4 — Power Out

Three-stage turbine of a GE J79 engine at the MTU Museum. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

04

The turbine
delivers power

Hot expanding gas blasts through turbine blades, spinning the shaft at thousands of RPM. This drives the compressor and produces useful output: thrust for a jet, shaft torque for a helicopter, or electricity from a generator.

Thousands of RPM Thrust · Power · Torque
