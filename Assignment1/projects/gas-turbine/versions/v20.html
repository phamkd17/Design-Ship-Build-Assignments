Stage 1 — Intake

Air enters the engine

Atmospheric air is drawn in through the front inlet and smoothed before compression. A large fan accelerates the air — a commercial turbofan swallows 1,000 kg/s at cruise.

Axial compressor — alternating rotor and stator blade rows. (Wikimedia Commons)

Stage 2 — Compression

Pressure rises to 40× atmospheric

Alternating rotating and fixed blade rows squeeze the air progressively. By the exit, pressure reaches 40× atmospheric — the biggest driver of engine efficiency.

Combustor of a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 3 — Combustion

Continuous flame at 1,500°C

Fuel burns without stopping at temperatures of 1,500°C. Hollow blades and ceramic coatings protect the turbine from heat that would otherwise destroy it.

Three-stage turbine of a GE J79 engine at the MTU Museum. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 4 — Power Out

Hot gas spins the turbine

Expanding exhaust blasts through turbine blades at thousands of RPM. The shaft drives the compressor and delivers the remainder as thrust, shaft torque, or generator output.

Two Types of Gas Turbine

Thrust Engine — Turbofan

A large fan pushes most air around the core as bypass flow, producing efficient thrust. Used in commercial jets and fighter aircraft.

Power Engine — Turboshaft

All turbine energy extracted as shaft power — drives generators, helicopter rotors, and ship propellers.
