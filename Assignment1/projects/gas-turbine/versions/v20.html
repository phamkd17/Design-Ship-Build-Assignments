Why It Matters

Combined heat and power (CHP) — also called cogeneration — is the most efficient use of any gas turbine. Instead of discarding 570°C exhaust as waste heat, a CHP plant routes it to a heat recovery system that produces steam, hot water, or process heat. The result: 70–85% total system efficiency, more than double the 35–40% of a conventional power plant. For a university campus, pharmaceutical plant, or district heating network, CHP reduces both energy costs and carbon emissions. As natural gas prices stabilize and carbon pricing grows, on-site CHP becomes even more attractive compared to buying grid power and separate boiler heat. The same gas turbine core that powers commercial jets is now the engine of the clean energy transition — one building at a time.

Stage 1 — Intake

CHP plant scale — 400–800 kg/s

Utility-scale CHP plants ingest 400–800 kg/s of air. Inlet cooling using recovered waste heat extends summer output by 10–15% — keeping generation stable when district heating demand also peaks. Inlet filter houses are sized for 25,000+ hours of operation without major service.

Axial compressor — alternating rotor and stator blade rows. (Wikimedia Commons)

Stage 2 — Compression

OPR 23:1 — balancing power and heat

CHP plants balance compressor OPR carefully: higher OPR 23:1 raises electrical efficiency but reduces exhaust temperature. Designers optimize the tradeoff between power output and HRSG steam quality — a district heating system needs 570°C exhaust, which informs turbine OPR selection from the start of the design process.

Combustor of a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 3 — Combustion

DLN combustors — H₂ co-firing

Urban CHP plants operate near emissions permit limits. DLN combustors maintain <25 ppm NOx while co-firing hydrogen blends up to 30%. By 2030, GE and Siemens target 100% hydrogen combustion — making today's CHP infrastructure the retrofit-ready foundation for zero-carbon district heating networks.

Three-stage turbine of a GE J79 engine at the MTU Museum. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 4 — Power Out

570°C exhaust → HRSG → heat

Instead of exhausting to atmosphere, 570°C exhaust flows into a heat recovery steam generator (HRSG). The HRSG produces steam for process use, district heating, or a second steam turbine — capturing 40%+ of fuel energy that simple-cycle generation discards, and pushing total CHP system efficiency above 80%.

Two Types of Gas Turbine

Simple-Cycle CHP

Gas turbine generator plus HRSG — turbine exhaust directly to heat recovery. Electrical efficiency ~35–42%, total system efficiency 70%+. Fast startup for buildings and campuses.

Combined-Cycle CHP (CCGT)

Exhaust to HRSG drives a steam turbine for additional power. Electrical efficiency 60%+, total CHP system efficiency 80%+. The highest-efficiency thermal generation configuration commercially available.
