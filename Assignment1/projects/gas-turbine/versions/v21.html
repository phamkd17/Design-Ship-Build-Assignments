"Every time you board a flight or switch on a light, there is a good chance a gas turbine made it possible."

The gas turbine is arguably the defining machine of the modern age. Its extraordinary power-to-weight ratio — producing thousands of horsepower from an engine small enough to fit in a living room — makes it irreplaceable anywhere size and weight are critical. Unlike a steam turbine, which takes hours to warm up, a gas turbine reaches full power in minutes, making it the world's most important tool for meeting sudden spikes in electricity demand. As renewables grow, gas turbines increasingly serve as the grid's shock absorber — spinning up instantly to balance intermittent wind and solar. Far from becoming obsolete, they continue to evolve through ceramic blade materials, additive manufacturing, and hydrogen combustion, positioning them as a bridge technology toward a lower-carbon future.

Where Gas Turbines Are Used

Thrust Production

The turbine's exhaust is directed rearward at high velocity to push the vehicle forward.

✈️
Commercial Aviation
Turbofan engines power nearly every commercial aircraft — from the 737 to the A380.
🪖
Military Aircraft
Fighter jets use afterburning turbofans to reach supersonic speeds.
🚀
Cruise Missiles
Small turbojets propel cruise missiles over long distances at high subsonic speed.
Power Production

The turbine shaft drives a generator, propeller, or pump instead of producing direct thrust.

⚡
Power Generation
Gas turbines produce ~20% of the world's electricity and spin up in minutes for peak demand.
🚢
Naval Ships
Destroyers and frigates use marine gas turbines for their rapid acceleration.
🚁
Helicopters
Turboshaft engines deliver shaft power to rotor systems at exceptional power-to-weight.
🛢️
Oil & Gas
Gas turbines drive compressor stations pushing gas through thousands of miles of pipeline.

Stage One

Air enters the engine

Everything begins with air. Atmospheric air is drawn into the engine through a carefully shaped inlet designed to slow and smooth the flow. In a commercial turbofan, a large fan at the front does much of the work, accelerating 1,000 kg/s of air at cruise altitude.

Axial compressor — alternating rotating and stationary blade rows. (Wikimedia Commons)

Stage Two

Air is squeezed under enormous pressure

Alternating rotating blades (rotors) and fixed blades (stators) compress the air progressively tighter. By the exit, pressure reaches 40× atmospheric — the single biggest lever on engine efficiency.

Combustor of a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage Three

Fuel burns — continuously, at extreme heat

Fuel is injected and ignited. Unlike a car engine's intermittent explosions, the flame burns continuously at 1,500°C. Hollow blades and ceramic coatings protect the turbine from heat that would otherwise destroy it.

Three-stage turbine of a GE J79 engine at the MTU Museum. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage Four

Hot gas spins the turbine — and drives the world

Expanding exhaust blasts through the turbine blades at immense velocity, spinning the shaft. Part drives the compressor, keeping the cycle self-sustaining. The remainder becomes thrust, shaft torque for a helicopter's rotor, or electrical power from a coupled generator — 60% efficiency in combined-cycle plants.

Two Types of Gas Turbine

Thrust Engine — Turbofan

A large fan pushes most air around the core as bypass flow, producing efficient thrust. Used in commercial jets and fighter aircraft.

Power Engine — Turboshaft

All turbine energy extracted as shaft power, driving a generator, helicopter rotor, or ship propeller.
