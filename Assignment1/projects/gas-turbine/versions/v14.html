Gas Turbines in Power Generation

Gas turbines produce roughly 20% of the world's electricity. Here's how they compare to alternatives across six dimensions that drive every utility's technology decision.

Why It Matters

Gas turbines generate roughly 20% of global electricity and are uniquely positioned for the energy transition. No other technology combines fast startup (under 10 minutes from cold), high power density, fuel flexibility — natural gas, hydrogen blends, biogas — and proven 60%+ combined-cycle efficiency at utility scale. For grid operators, they solve two problems at once: reliable baseload power and fast-response peaking when demand surges. As solar and wind capacity grows, gas turbines increasingly serve as the dispatchable backbone that makes high renewable penetration viable. Modern machines like the GE 7HA and Siemens H-class maintain capacity factors above 90% while ramping from 20% to 100% load in under 30 minutes — a flexibility no large nuclear or coal plant can match.

Choose a Gas Turbine when…

  • Power-to-weight ratio is critical (aviation, helicopters)
  • You need rapid startup for peak power demand
  • Large-scale continuous power generation is required
  • Smooth, low-vibration operation is essential (naval ships)
  • Fuel flexibility and future H₂ compatibility matter

Choose a Piston Engine when…

  • Power output is modest (cars, light aircraft, small boats)
  • Cost and simplicity of maintenance are top priorities
  • Frequent start-stop cycles are required
  • Fuel is standard petrol or diesel only
  • A proven, widely-understood technology is needed

Simple-Cycle vs. Combined-Cycle

Simple-Cycle

Simple-cycle gas turbine — fast startup for grid demand response, peaking, and spinning reserve. Full output in under 10 minutes.

Combined-Cycle (CCGT)

Combined-cycle (CCGT) — exhaust heat drives a steam turbine via HRSG. 60%+ electrical efficiency. The workhorse of modern utility grids.
