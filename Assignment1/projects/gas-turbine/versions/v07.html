Stage 1 — Intake

Air enters the engine

Atmospheric air is drawn in through the inlet at the front. A large fan — or simply the inlet shape — smooths and accelerates the flow. One commercial turbofan swallows over 1,000 kg of air every second at cruise altitude.

Sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan — intake and fan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

02

Stage 2 — Compression

Air is squeezed under pressure

Alternating rows of rotating and fixed blades progressively compress the air to up to 40 times atmospheric pressure. This dense, energised air is the secret to the engine's power — more pressure means more energy released per kilogram of fuel.

Axial compressor — rotor and stator stages compress air in sequence. (Wikimedia Commons)

03

Stage 3 — Combustion

Fuel burns continuously

Fuel is injected and ignited in the combustor. The flame burns continuously at over 1,500 °C — far above what any metal can survive unprotected. Elaborate cooling systems channel air through hollow blades and thermal barrier coatings to keep the hardware intact.

Combustor of a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

04

Stage 4 — Power Extraction

Hot gas spins the turbine

Expanding exhaust blasts through turbine blades at thousands of RPM. The turbine drives the compressor via a shared shaft — keeping the cycle self-sustaining. Remaining energy is converted to thrust, shaft power for a generator, or rotor torque for a helicopter, depending on the application.

Three-stage turbine of a GE J79 engine. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)
