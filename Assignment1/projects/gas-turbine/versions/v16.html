The turboshaft gas turbine delivers hundreds to thousands of horsepower from an engine small enough to hold in two hands. From the Black Hawk to the Dauphin, every modern helicopter depends on this four-stage thermodynamic cycle.

Why It Matters

Helicopter turboshaft engines represent the highest power density aviation powerplant ever produced at scale. The GE T700 powering the Black Hawk produces 1,800 shp from an engine weighing just 198 kg — a power-to-weight ratio exceeding 6 kW/kg. This extraordinary density makes the gas turbine the only viable choice for helicopter propulsion at anything beyond light aircraft sizes. Turboshafts start faster, run smoother, and tolerate ingesting dust, sand, and salt spray far better than piston alternatives at equivalent power levels. Modern turboshafts like the Safran Arrius and P&WC PW200 operate above 15,000 ft with in-flight restart capability — critical for search and rescue and high-altitude military operations. As unmanned aerial systems grow, miniaturized turboshaft derivatives are powering heavy-lift cargo drones and VTOL UAVs where battery energy density falls short.

Stage One — Intake

Sand-filtered air — 10 kg/s

A helicopter turboshaft draws 8–15 kg/s of filtered air — a fraction of a fixed-wing engine. The inlet particle separator is critical for desert and ship-deck operations. The GE T700's separator extends MTBO from 600 hours in sandy environments to over 3,500 hours — the difference between viable operating economics and a grounded fleet.

↳ Particle separator — T700/T701C

Stage Two — Compression

Axial + centrifugal, OPR 17:1

Helicopter compressors combine axial + centrifugal stages — axial stages build pressure efficiently; one centrifugal stage adds the final ratio without extra length. The GE T700 achieves OPR 17:1 in six stages, maintaining adequate airflow at density altitudes up to 15,000+ ft where air is half as dense as at sea level.

↳ Centrifugal stage — compact for narrow rotor bay

Stage Three — Combustion

Annular combustor at altitude

Helicopter combustors use an annular (ring-shaped) design — maximum combustion volume in the smallest diameter, essential for a narrow engine bay. Combustion must stay stable where air density halves at 20,000 ft. The T700 maintains reliable in-flight relight capability at altitudes where larger engines struggle — critical for autorotation recovery and SAR missions.

↳ Stable at 20,000 ft — in-flight restart

Stage Four — Power Out

Free turbine → gearbox → 350 RPM

A free-power turbine, spinning separately from the gas generator, extracts all shaft energy and delivers it to a gearbox. This design keeps rotor speed constant even as engine power varies — essential for rotor system control and autorotation safety. The gearbox steps 6,000–30,000 RPM down to 350–400 RPM at the main rotor while tripling output torque.

↳ Free turbine → gearbox → 350 RPM rotor

Turboshaft Engine Architecture

Thrust Engine

Gas generator core — same Brayton cycle, but exhaust thrust is replaced by a free power turbine driving the output shaft.

Power Engine

Turboshaft — free power turbine drives main rotor via gearbox (6,000→350 RPM). GE T700, Safran Arrius, P&WC PW200.
