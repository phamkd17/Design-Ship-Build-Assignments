The gas turbine converts chemical energy into mechanical work through a continuous four-stage process. Every flight, every city's lights, every warship at sea depends on this cycle repeating millions of times per minute.

Stage One — Intake

Air enters the engine

Atmospheric air is drawn in through the front inlet. The intake duct is designed to slow and smooth the airflow before compression begins. In a turbofan, a large fan at the front handles much of the work — a single commercial engine can swallow over 1,000 kg per second at cruise altitude.

↳ 1,000 kg of air per second

Stage Two — Compression

Air squeezed to 40× pressure

The axial compressor is a sequence of alternating rotating blades (rotors) and fixed blades (stators). Each stage increases pressure. By the compressor exit, air reaches 40× atmospheric pressure. The higher the pressure ratio, the more energy is unlocked from each kilogram of fuel burned.

↳ 40× atmospheric pressure

Stage Three — Combustion

Continuous flame at 1,500°C

Fuel is injected and ignited in the combustion chamber. The flame burns continuously — unlike the intermittent explosions in a piston engine. Temperatures reach 1,500°C, far beyond what any unprotected metal can survive. Hollow blades and ceramic thermal barrier coatings make it possible.

↳ 1,500°C — hotter than lava

Stage Four — Power Out

Hot gas drives the turbine

Expanding exhaust blasts through turbine blades at thousands of RPM. The turbine shaft drives the compressor — keeping the cycle self-sustaining. Whatever energy remains is the useful output: jet thrust, shaft power for a generator, or rotor torque for a helicopter.

↳ Thrust · Electricity · Shaft torque
