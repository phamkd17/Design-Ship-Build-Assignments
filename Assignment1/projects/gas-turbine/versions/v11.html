How a Gas Turbine Works

Scroll through the four stages that turn air and fuel into power.

Why It Matters

Gas turbines are one of the most consequential technologies in modern civilization. Every time you board a flight, switch on a light, or heat your home, there is a good chance a gas turbine made it possible. Their extraordinary power-to-weight ratio — producing thousands of horsepower from an engine you could fit in a living room — makes them irreplaceable in applications where size and weight are critical. Unlike steam turbines, which take hours to reach operating temperature, a gas turbine can reach full power in minutes, making it the backbone of electrical grids worldwide for meeting sudden spikes in demand. As the global energy mix shifts toward renewables, gas turbines are taking on a new role: balancing intermittent wind and solar power by providing instant, reliable backup generation. Far from becoming obsolete, the gas turbine continues to evolve — with advances in ceramic blade materials, additive manufacturing, and hydrogen combustion positioning it as a key bridge technology toward a lower-carbon future.

Where Gas Turbines Are Used

Thrust Production

The turbine's exhaust is directed rearward at high velocity to push the vehicle forward.

✈️
Commercial Aviation
Turbofan engines power nearly every commercial aircraft — from the Boeing 737 to the Airbus A380.
🪖
Military Aircraft
Fighter jets use turbojet and afterburning turbofan engines to reach supersonic speeds.
🚀
Cruise Missiles
Small turbofan or turbojet engines propel cruise missiles over long distances at high subsonic speeds.
Power Production

The turbine shaft drives a generator, propeller, or pump instead of producing direct thrust.

⚡
Power Generation
Gas turbines produce ~20% of the world's electricity and can spin up in minutes for peak demand.
🚢
Naval Ships
Destroyers and frigates use marine gas turbines for their high power-to-weight ratio and rapid acceleration.
🚁
Helicopters
Turboshaft engines deliver shaft power to rotor systems with exceptional power-to-weight performance.
🛢️
Oil & Gas Pipelines
Gas turbines drive compressor stations that push natural gas through thousands of miles of pipeline.

Stage 1 — Intake

Air enters the engine

Atmospheric air is drawn in through the front inlet. The intake duct is shaped to smooth the airflow before compression begins. A large fan accelerates the air — a commercial turbofan swallows 1,000 kg/s at cruise.

Axial compressor — alternating rotor and stator blade rows. (Wikimedia Commons)

Stage 2 — Compression

Air squeezed to 40× pressure

Alternating rotating and fixed blade rows compress the air progressively. By the end of the compressor, pressure reaches 40× atmospheric. Higher pressure means more energy from the same amount of fuel.

Combustor of a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 3 — Combustion

Continuous flame at 1,500°C

Fuel is injected and ignited. Unlike a piston engine, the flame burns continuously. Temperatures exceed 1,500°C — hotter than most metals can survive without advanced hollow-blade cooling and ceramic coatings.

Three-stage turbine of a GE J79 engine at the MTU Museum. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 4 — Power Out

Hot gas spins the turbine

Expanding exhaust blasts through turbine blades at thousands of RPM, spinning the shaft. The shaft drives the compressor at the front, keeping the cycle running. Remaining energy becomes thrust, generator output, or rotor torque.

Two Types of Gas Turbine

Thrust Engine

Turbofan — a large fan pushes most air around the core as bypass flow. Used in commercial jets and fighter aircraft.

Power Engine

Turboshaft — all turbine energy extracted as shaft power, driving a generator. Used in power plants, naval ships, and oil & gas facilities.
