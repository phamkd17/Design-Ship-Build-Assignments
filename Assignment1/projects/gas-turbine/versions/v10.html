How a Gas Turbine Works

Scroll through the four stages.

Sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan — intake and fan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 1 — Intake

Air enters the engine

Atmospheric air is drawn in through the front inlet. The intake is carefully shaped to smooth the airflow before compression.

1,000 kg/s of air swallowed per second by a commercial turbofan at cruise altitude.

Stage 2 — Compression

Air is squeezed under pressure

Alternating rotating and fixed blade rows progressively compress the air, stage by stage.

40× atmospheric pressure — the tighter the squeeze, the more power per kg of fuel.

Axial compressor — alternating rotor and stator blade rows. (Wikimedia Commons)

Combustor of a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 3 — Combustion

Fuel burns continuously

Fuel is injected and ignited. Unlike a piston engine, combustion in a gas turbine never stops — a sustained flame burns constantly.

1,500 °C — hotter than most metals can survive without advanced cooling.

Stage 4 — Power Out

Hot gas spins the turbine

Expanding exhaust blasts through turbine blades, spinning the shaft at thousands of RPM. The shaft drives the compressor — and delivers useful work: thrust, electrical power, or rotor torque.

60% efficiency in combined-cycle power plants — best in class.
