Engineering Explained

One of the most powerful engines ever built — in four stages.

Gas turbines power the planes we fly in, the electricity in our homes, and the ships that guard our seas. Here is how they work.

"Every time you board a flight or switch on a light, there is a good chance a gas turbine made it possible."

The gas turbine is arguably the defining machine of the modern age. Its extraordinary power-to-weight ratio — producing thousands of horsepower from an engine small enough to fit in a living room — makes it irreplaceable anywhere size and weight are critical. Unlike a steam turbine, which takes hours to warm up, a gas turbine reaches full power in minutes, making it the world's most important tool for meeting sudden spikes in electricity demand.

Stage One

Air enters the engine

Sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan — intake and fan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Everything begins with air. Atmospheric air is drawn into the engine through the inlet — a carefully shaped duct designed to slow and smooth the flow. In a commercial turbofan, a large fan at the front does much of the work, accelerating 1,000 kg/s of air at cruise.

Stage Two

Air is squeezed under enormous pressure

Axial compressor — alternating rotating and stationary blade rows. (Wikimedia Commons)

The air passes through an axial compressor — a series of alternating rotating blades (rotors) and fixed blades (stators) that squeeze it progressively tighter. By the end of the compression stage, the air has been compressed to 40× atmospheric pressure. The more tightly compressed the air, the more energy can be extracted from each kilogram of fuel — compression ratio is the single biggest lever on engine efficiency.

Stage Three

Fuel burns — continuously, at extreme heat

Combustor of a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Fuel — jet fuel, natural gas, or increasingly hydrogen — is injected and ignited. Unlike a car engine's intermittent explosions, the flame in a gas turbine burns continuously at 1,500 °C. The turbine blades downstream survive this inferno only through hollow cooling passages and ceramic thermal barrier coatings — among the most sophisticated manufacturing achievements in engineering.

Stage Four

Hot gas spins the turbine — and drives the world

Three-stage turbine of a GE J79 engine at the MTU Museum. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Expanding exhaust gas blasts through the turbine blades at immense velocity, spinning the turbine shaft. Part of that rotation drives the compressor at the front — keeping the cycle self-sustaining. The remainder is captured as useful work: thrust out the back of a jet engine, shaft torque to a helicopter's rotor, or electrical power from a coupled generator.
