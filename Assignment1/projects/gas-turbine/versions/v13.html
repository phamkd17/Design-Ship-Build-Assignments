Why It Matters

As solar and wind capacity surges, grid operators face a relentless challenge: the sun does not always shine and the wind does not always blow. Gas turbines are the essential complement — starting from cold in under 10 minutes, ramping at over 100 MW per minute, and capable of co-firing hydrogen blends as the fuel mix evolves. Modern combined-cycle gas turbine (CCGT) plants already exceed 60% thermal efficiency — the highest of any dispatchable generation technology. Peaker units cover demand spikes that batteries cannot yet economically fill. As storage costs fall, gas turbines will shift from baseload toward backup and frequency regulation — but the physics of energy density mean they remain the grid's most reliable insurance policy for decades to come.

Stage 1 — Intake

Filtered air — up to 800 kg/s

Utility-scale gas turbines ingest 400–800 kg/s through multi-stage inlet filter houses. Evaporative coolers or mechanical chillers lower inlet temperature by 10–15°C, recovering 15%+ of output lost on hot summer days when grid demand peaks highest.

Sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan — intake and fan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 2 — Compression

OPR 23:1 — the efficiency lever

The GE 7HA.03 achieves an overall pressure ratio of OPR 23.5:1 across 14 stages. Inter-stage bleed air supports DLN combustor premixing — critical for meeting EPA Tier 4 and EU IED NOx limits that grid operators must demonstrate to regulators.

Axial compressor — rotor and stator blade rows. (Wikimedia Commons)

Stage 3 — Combustion

DLN burners — <25 ppm NOx

Dry Low-NOx (DLN) combustors premix fuel and air before ignition, holding flame temperature precisely at TIT 1,400°C. H₂ co-firing up to 30% is commercially deployed today; 100% hydrogen combustion is in pilot testing for 2030 fleet availability.

Combustor of a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 4 — Power Out

400–600 MW per CCGT train

The power turbine drives a generator at 3,000 or 3,600 RPM, producing up to 400 MW per block. In CCGT configuration, 570°C exhaust feeds a heat recovery steam generator (HRSG), adding 200 MW more and pushing plant efficiency above 63%. Grid response from standby to full load: under 30 minutes.

Three-stage turbine of a GE J79 engine at the MTU Museum. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Simple-Cycle vs. Combined-Cycle

Peaker (Simple-Cycle)

Open-cycle turbine — spins up from cold in under 10 min for fast grid response. Efficiency ~38%.

CCGT (Combined-Cycle)

CCGT plant — HRSG recovers exhaust heat to drive a steam turbine. Efficiency 60–63%+.
