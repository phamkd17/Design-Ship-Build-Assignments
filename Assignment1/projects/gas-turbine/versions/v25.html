Engineering Explained

One of humanity's most powerful engines — in four stages.

Gas turbines power the flights we take, the electricity in our homes, and the ships that guard our seas. Here is how they work.

"Every time you board a flight or switch on a light, there is a good chance a gas turbine made it possible."

The gas turbine is arguably the defining machine of the modern age. Its extraordinary power-to-weight ratio — producing thousands of horsepower from an engine small enough to fit in a living room — makes it irreplaceable anywhere size and weight are critical. In commercial aviation, high-bypass turbofans have cut fuel burn by 40% since 1990. In military aircraft, afterburning turbojets push fighters past Mach 2. In power generation and renewable grid balancing, gas turbines spin up in under ten minutes to absorb demand spikes that batteries cannot yet fill. Naval destroyers sprint past 30 knots on marine gas turbines; helicopter turboshafts deliver shaft power at altitudes where piston engines fail; oil and gas pipeline compressors run continuously for 25,000+ hours on direct mechanical drive. Far from becoming obsolete, the gas turbine continues to evolve — through ceramic blade materials, additive manufacturing, and hydrogen combustion — remaining the engine at the center of modern civilization.

Why It Matters

Gas turbines serve seven distinct industries — each demanding something different from the same four-stage thermodynamic cycle. In commercial aviation, high-bypass turbofans achieve bypass ratios of 12:1 and TSFC below 0.50 kg/kN·h. Military fighter jets add afterburners for 180 kN of thrust. Renewable energy peaker plants ramp at 100 MW per minute to balance wind and solar. Utility CCGT plants exceed 63% thermal efficiency. Naval destroyers rely on marine gas turbines to sprint past 30 knots in under two minutes. Helicopter turboshafts deliver free-shaft-power at 15,000 ft where piston engines cannot. And oil and gas pipeline compressors run 25,000+ hours on direct mechanical drive with API 616 fuel conditioning. Select an application above to see how each stage changes.

Engine Cross-Section

Turbofan — Commercial Aviation

Stage 1 — Intake

Large fan ingests 800+ kg/s

A high-bypass turbofan's large front fan accelerates 800 kg/s of air, with ~85% bypassing the core as cold thrust stream. The inlet duct is precisely contoured to avoid flow separation at all speeds and angles of attack. Bypass ratio (BPR 12:1 on CFM LEAP) is the primary driver of fuel economy — the higher the ratio, the more thrust per kilogram of fuel burned.

    Sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan — large front fan and bypass duct. High BPR (12:1 on CFM LEAP) is the primary driver of commercial aviation fuel economy. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

    Stage 2 — Compression

    17-stage HPC — OPR 40:1

    The high-pressure compressor squeezes air to 40× atmospheric across 17 stages (CFM56/LEAP). Variable inlet guide vanes and stator vanes adjust to maintain surge margin across the full flight envelope — from sea-level takeoff to Mach 0.85 cruise at 12,000 m. Tip clearance control via active thermal management keeps efficiency above 90%.

      Axial compressor — 17+ alternating rotor and stator rows compress air to 40× atmospheric in a modern turbofan. Variable inlet guide vanes maintain surge margin across the flight envelope. (Wikimedia Commons)

      Stage 3 — Combustion

      Annular combustor at 1,600°C TIT

      An annular combustor burns Jet-A or SAF continuously at a turbine inlet temperature of 1,600°C — far above nickel alloy melting point. Transpiration and film cooling plus thermal barrier coatings protect HPT blades. Modern TAPS (Twin Annular Pre-Swirl) combustors achieve ultra-low NOx while maintaining combustion stability from idle to max thrust.

        Annular combustor — burns Jet-A or SAF continuously at 1,600°C TIT. TAPS (Twin Annular Pre-Swirl) design achieves ultra-low NOx while maintaining stability from idle to max thrust. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

        Stage 4 — Power Out

        HPT drives core; LPT drives fan

        A single-stage HPT (TBC-coated, cooled) extracts energy to drive the HPC on the high-speed shaft. A multi-stage LPT on the low-speed shaft drives the large front fan, producing ~75–100 kN of net thrust. The cold bypass stream and hot core exhaust mix at the nozzle, with TSFC of 0.50 kg/kN·h — the best in class for long-haul aircraft.

          Three-stage turbine — single HPT drives the HPC, multi-stage LPT drives the front fan. Together they deliver 75–100 kN net thrust at TSFC 0.50 kg/kN·h. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)
