How a Gas Turbine Works

One of humanity's most powerful engines — explained in four stages.

Sectioned GE J85-GE-17A turbojet engine — you can see all four stages from left to right. (Wikimedia Commons / Public Domain)

Air intake and fan of a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. Air enters from the left. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 01 — Intake

Air enters the engine

Atmospheric air is drawn in through the inlet at the front of the engine. A large fan accelerates massive volumes of air — a commercial jet engine can swallow over 1,000 kg of air per second. The intake is shaped to slow and smooth the airflow before compression.

Axial compressor animation — alternating rotating (rotor) and stationary (stator) blade rows. (Wikimedia Commons)

Stage 02 — Compression

Air is squeezed under pressure

Rows of rotating blades (rotors) and fixed blades (stators) work together to progressively compress the incoming air. Pressure can rise to 40× atmospheric. Compressed air holds far more chemical energy potential — the tighter the squeeze, the more power available from the same amount of fuel.

Combustor from a sectioned Rolls-Royce Turbomeca Adour turbofan. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 03 — Combustion

Fuel burns continuously

Jet fuel or natural gas is injected into the combustion chamber and ignited. Unlike a car engine, combustion in a gas turbine is continuous — a sustained flame burns at temperatures exceeding 1,500 °C. The burning gases expand rapidly, generating a high-pressure, high-velocity stream of hot exhaust.

Three-stage turbine of a GE J79 engine at the MTU Museum. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA)

Stage 04 — Power Extraction

Hot gas spins the turbine

The high-energy exhaust gas blasts through turbine blades, spinning them at thousands of revolutions per minute. This rotation drives the compressor at the front (keeping the cycle going) and — depending on the application — also spins a generator for electricity, a propeller for a ship or aircraft, or produces raw thrust for a jet.
